# Computational approaches in neuroscience

<b>Biophysical and mechanistic modeling</b> starts from physics and chemistry. Hodgkin-Huxley–style models simulate how ion channels open and close to produce action potentials. Compartmental models extend this to entire dendritic trees, capturing how signals integrate across a neuron's morphology. At the largest scale, network simulations wire thousands to millions of model neurons together to reproduce circuit-level phenomena like cortical oscillations or sensory processing.

<b>Neural coding and information theory</b> asks: what does a spike train mean? Spike train analysis characterizes the statistical structure of neural firing — rate codes, temporal codes, burst patterns. Information-theoretic tools (entropy, mutual information) quantify how much a neuron or population tells you about a stimulus. Population decoding flips the question: given activity from many neurons, can we reconstruct what the animal saw, decided, or intended?

<b>Dynamical systems and network theory</b> treats neural activity as a trajectory through a high-dimensional state space. Attractor networks explain how stable patterns (like a memory or a decision) persist. Dimensionality reduction (PCA, GPFA, UMAP) finds the low-dimensional manifolds that neural populations actually traverse. Recurrent dynamics analysis characterizes oscillations, stability, and how perturbations propagate through circuits.

<b>Normative and Bayesian approaches</b> work top-down: rather than simulating mechanisms, they ask what computation the brain should perform to solve a task optimally. Bayesian inference and predictive coding propose that the brain maintains probabilistic beliefs and updates them with sensory evidence. Reinforcement learning models connect reward prediction errors to dopamine signals. Efficient coding theory asks how sensory systems should be organized to transmit maximal information given metabolic constraints.

<b>Data-driven and machine learning</b> approaches have exploded with modern recording technologies. Encoding models predict neural responses from stimuli using regression or GLMs. Deep neural network comparison asks whether artificial networks trained on tasks develop representations that resemble those in biological brain areas. Latent variable models (like LFADS or switching linear dynamical systems) infer hidden computational states from noisy, high-dimensional recordings.

Connectomics and graph theory maps the physical wiring — from electron-microscopy reconstructions of every synapse in small volumes to diffusion-MRI tractography of whole-brain white matter. Graph-theoretic measures (degree, modularity, small-worldness, rich clubs) then characterize how these networks are organized and what that organization implies for function.
